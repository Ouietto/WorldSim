# Asabiya 0.7 — transport, local power and political continuity

This version connects settlement, warfare, administration, reform and collapse
to shared resources. It is an exploratory historical model. Its rules are
explicit hypotheses, rather than established universal laws of social change.


## Changes in 0.7

### Herds, traction and carrying capacity

Six livestock populations are separate from technical knowledge: cattle,
water buffalo, donkeys, horses, camels and llamas. They have different traction,
pack and riding contributions, habitat suitability and breeding rates.
Historical mode uses approximate domestication scenario anchors; generated
worlds choose suitable origins. These dates and locations are not a new
archaeological chronology. Knowing riding or herding does not create breeders.
Founder placement searches for a viable nearby habitat. River water can support
buffalo in an otherwise dry district, and camels can use arid scrub that is not
temperate pasture.
Animals diffuse through staged local exchange and trade links, with food
payments transferred between households. Breeding and exchange phases have a separate conservation ledger;
settlement and migration transfers are checked by focused scenarios. Settlers, displaced groups and mobile coalitions carry actual
animals from their origins. Animal breeding and exchange run in five-year
steps, so new animal availability can take years to affect a route.

Fodder competes with household food and is explicitly subtracted in the annual
food ledger. Grazing suitability, crowding, weather and feed shortages affect
herd survival. Draft capacity limits plough benefits. Pack supply lowers the
population and occupation requirements of a frontier; boats and organized
human labor provide alternatives. Forest is a transport and husbandry cost,
not a universal livestock exclusion. Buffalo can support wet Asian agriculture.
Earth's coarse tsetse habitat proxy is confined to mainland Africa; generated
worlds retain a hypothetical climate-defined disease habitat.

### Communication corridors and natural frontiers

Order delivery, replies and bulk supplies follow separate directed routes.
Distances are estimated travel days, quantized into half-day steps. Routing
uses maintained roads, real remount availability, relay knowledge, terrain,
river direction and coastal shipping. Local river craft can predate developed
seafaring. Major channels are inferred from drainage, gradient and roughness;
low flow, seasonal rainfall and cold reduce reliability. Canal knowledge can
help river bottlenecks. This remains a coarse grid proxy, not a surveyed map
of navigable channels or simulated monthly vessel movements.

A state's reach depends on round-trip response and supply burden, rather than
one universal geographic radius. Connected waterways create demand for
central offices and standardization. They do not exempt a polity from budgets,
local bargaining or succession. Mountains and transverse river crossings
increase attack and travel costs. Longitudinal river movement can be cheap at
the same location. Bridges reduce crossing friction. Defensible frontiers
require fewer recurring occupation personnel; valley routes and passes remain
potential approaches. No named river, mountain range or region receives a
scripted frontier or cohesion bonus.

### City states, leagues and conquest

Urban polities with small territorial hinterlands can be classified as city
states. Urban populations, civic identities and memories of previous local
rule persist through conquest. They lower the initial ability of an outsider
to integrate a city. Alliances of small states can become leagues that retain
local councils and laws; these are tributary/federal approximations, not a
complete model of a historical constitution. Membership can lapse if relative
power or protection changes. Leagues are not silently annexed by the ordinary
vassal-absorption rule.

Acquiring provinces creates conquest obligations. Recent, poorly integrated
populations provide few recruits. Obligations decay through time, reliable
services and bureaucratic integration, while increasing fiscal strain. The
death of a successful conqueror can expose personal arrangements that were
not converted into trusted succession institutions. Fragmentation can restore
local political identities or produce successor governments. A city-state era
is an emergent regional possibility, not a mandatory global sequence.

### Peace, institutional memory and elite interests

Preparedness, retained crisis experience and frontier intelligence are separate
stocks. Training consumes provisions. Record keeping, administrative schools
and continuity preserve knowledge; maintained preparation can improve during
peace. Severe losses remove expertise. There is no fixed old-age penalty or
rule that stable borders inevitably cause collapse. Raiding coalitions learn
about particular opponents, but knowledge fades and repeated defeats lower a
target's appeal. Skills have ceilings and both sides learn.

Provincial landholders have local stakes and loyalties. Reliable protection,
rights and secure property can make them defend the existing order. Failed
protection and credible rival terms can induce a local change of allegiance.
This transfers jurisdiction and a limited actual contingent, not ethnicity or
population. Retained local authority strengthens intermediaries and limits
central extraction. Existing costly reforms can replace some of that power
only when supporting institutions and coalitions exist. Elites and estates
remain aggregate provincial proxies, not individually simulated families.

### Pastoral coalitions and migration

Pastoral territories are difficult for sedentary armies to occupy, especially
when residents can evade them using herds and riding animals. Dense urban
agricultural enclaves remain possible. Nomadic coalitions can form around local
organization, pasture conditions, threats and access to settled wealth.
Spatial clan groups track membership and loyalty; failed protection and reward
distribution can produce departures and successor coalitions. They have no
fixed lifespan. The spatial groups are political proxies, not a reconstruction
of kinship. Nomad-born rulers can still become settled conquest dynasties.

Raiding failures can redirect attacks or encourage migration. Migrating groups
explore accessible pasture corridors and local opportunities instead of knowing
all world capitals. Movement costs food, entails casualties, carries animals,
and can recruit actual households from communities along the route. Mobile
coalitions retain multiple population cohorts, can change direction, and can
persist for decades. Group identity is not treated as proof of uniform ancestry.
Repeated pressure can destabilize states without guaranteeing their defeat.

States can admit newcomers when they have room, provisions and a political
basis for agreement. An initial grant and annual provisions draw from actual
reserves. Fulfilled agreements support limited auxiliary recruitment. Sustained
broken promises can generate unrest and an armed departure drawn from the
settled population. Admission, redirection, negotiated settlement, violence and
state formation are distinct possible outcomes. Cohort ancestry/culture is
still compressed into the existing two-component provincial representation.

### Coastal settlement and map display

Mild, adequately watered coastal districts can support investment in productive
export crops. Maintained water transport, local nodes, urban demand, public
investment and time determine its benefit. This makes suitable North African
coasts attractive under the same rules as comparable coasts elsewhere. It
does not turn desert into farmland or treat inhabited land as empty.

City display can be hidden, restricted to capitals, limited to major regional
cities plus capitals, or expanded to all surviving towns. Importance uses
estimated urban population rather than rural province population. Government
seats remain visible in capital modes. Symbols and names avoid overlaps;
small-city names require zooming in. Filters change presentation only.

## Food, labour and population

One food unit is one person's annual ration. Provinces have a potential
carrying capacity, an actual annual harvest, household provisions, children and
a nutrition level. Harvests depend on available workers and local capacity;
knowing farming does not immediately produce a full-capacity harvest. Soldiers,
officials and fleet crews draw labour away from production.

Governments collect a portion of actual production. Collection depends on
administrative reach, integration, local autonomy, compliance and the resources
that can be appropriated. Livestock and labour contributions remain possible
without cereal farming. Taxes leave household stores and enter public stores.
Salaries return food to the same population counted in household consumption.
Tribute transfers existing provisions rather than producing an overlord bonus.

Stores spoil. Public reserves pay salaries, maintenance, relief, training,
campaign provisioning, colonies and reform. Insufficient revenue produces
arrears and unpaid services. Relief has a delivery cost and can fail to reach
distant provinces. Food conservation is checked across each annual economy
pass, including fodder, transportation losses and spoilage.

Births, ordinary deaths, starvation and an eighteen-year maturation pipeline
replace the single logistic population update. Scarce food reduces growth;
children cannot immediately become recruits or trained officials. People in
migration parties and displaced groups are included in population statistics.

## Settlements and frontiers

Migrants are removed from their actual home populations. Provisions, seed and
establishment inputs are deducted before departure. Clearing forest, difficult
terrain and longer journeys cost more. Newcomers produce only part of their
potential harvest during establishment; their presence does not suppress the
entire established population's harvest. Underfed colonies can fail and return
people to their home region.

Households can migrate independently. States can finance larger colonies and
must have enough local population, collected provisions, administrative
authority, service reliability and occupation capacity to sponsor a frontier.
Sea crossings require provisioning too. Claiming an empty cell no longer
creates settlers. A large population somewhere in an empire is insufficient
when provisions and protection cannot be delivered to the frontier.

A state with spare administrative capacity can also sponsor colonists after
conquering an inhabited frontier. This moves and provisions actual households;
jurisdiction alone does not replace the local population. The settlement counter
counts newly peopled provinces, while sponsored movements also include settlement
among existing inhabitants. The grid cannot distinguish every new village
inside a province roughly a hundred kilometres across.

Conquest of an existing community and founding an empty settlement are different
operations. Forests, rough country, local organization and disease resist
occupation. Cereal share is no longer a near-veto on conquest or retention.
Integration, administration and recurring occupation commitments constrain
expansion into populated territory.

## Trade and communication

Ports, navigable rivers, confluences, maintained road junctions, cities and
caravan stops create trade nodes. A network is rebuilt periodically from these
conditions. Small local exchanges are possible away from nodes, with much lower
capacity. River and maritime links allow larger flows. Actual food moves from
stocks with surplus to places with demand, subject to throughput, safety,
transport losses and tolls. Wars interrupt links.

Subsistence practices can spread locally without a long-distance commercial
network. The transmission of more specialized skills is more dependent on
nodes, contact, maintained routes and actual exchange. This avoids making the
spread of farming depend on already-existing cities or states.

This remains a food-equivalent economy. It does not separately simulate prices,
credit contracts, ore deposits or inventories of every traded commodity.
River routes are simplified, and passing through several exchange links is
resolved within an annual step rather than as individual caravans and ships.

## Communities, conquest and cultural change

Communal organization builds through repeated investment, surplus, shared
infrastructure and coordination needs. Councils, granaries and local
sanctuaries can exist outside states. Formal polities emerge only after local
organization and a supportable population develop. Council, temple and
patrimonial governments can start with different political arrangements.

Alliances of non-state communities can raid and conquer neighbours, including
state provinces. They gather local contingents and provisions, face resistance,
take losses, displace residents and move actual settlers. Successful warbands
can launch larger coercive migrations. Migration parties negotiate or fight;
settling in one location does not automatically annex neighbouring provinces.

Displaced people retain food and remain accounted for while they await movement.
They may migrate, suffer mortality or resettle. Hordes moving out withdraw
people, children and supplies from their former territory.

Language/cultural affiliation and ancestry are recorded separately. Settlement
can change both; assimilation can change culture without rewriting ancestry.
Culture changes by shares and can form hybrids. A culture can lose its final
communities through repeated displacement, mortality and assimilation, without
a universal extermination event or a scripted Indo-European replacement.
Ancestry is a coarse two-component population label, not a genetic simulation.

Political identity can persist across language boundaries. A state's heirs
inherit its political tradition; unrelated populations can participate through
long integration and reliable institutions. Safe interiors do not automatically
lose all cohesion: trust, negotiated rights and maintained administration can
support cooperation.

## Institutions, coalitions and succession

Added techniques include communal storage, accounts and record keeping,
censuses and land surveys, messenger relays, salaried public offices,
recognized succession procedures, negotiated charters, navigable canals and
schools for administrators. Record keeping includes non-alphabetic alternatives.
The older universal bronze → writing → wheel → iron prerequisite chain has
been relaxed.

Technologies permit capabilities; trained personnel and recurring support
determine how much is realized. Bureaucratic skill builds gradually. Roads and
canals are maintained stocks, distinct from knowledge of construction.
Administrative distance includes terrain, roads, rivers and shipping. A
connected transport network creates incentives for standards and central
offices, without named bonuses for China or the Mediterranean.

Nobles, officials, merchants and clergy have different sources of power and
different grounds for supporting government. Hereditary intermediaries provide
local coordination and military support while retaining revenue and limiting
central collection. Negotiated rights can improve loyalty while constraining
the ruler. Provincial organization and communications can support defection
when salaries, protection and central authority fail.

A reforming ruler needs competence, reserves, trained replacements, a
supporting coalition and an opportunity. Reform consumes substantial provisions
and initially disrupts the state. Its changes to offices, authority and
intermediary power outlive the individual ruler. Reform is uncommon, and it
does not guarantee permanent success. State revenue and total economic output
are separate measures.

Succession procedures can sustain hereditary continuity; councils select among
candidates. Low legitimacy, excluded elites and weak supporting coalitions
increase usurpation risk. A dynastic overthrow can leave the territorial and
administrative state intact.

Coalitions are aggregate organized groups rather than individually simulated
households and estates. The model does not yet maintain a complete genealogy,
legal system, named provincial governor population or historical constitution.

## Warfare, fleets and collapse

Armies recruit gradually from an integrated and locally supported adult recruitment base. Recently conquered provinces contribute very few recruits. Frontier occupation also requires spare trained personnel or local intermediaries after existing administrative commitments are met; cash alone cannot create unlimited occupation capacity. Offensive forces
and defensive reserves are finite allocations for the current year. A
contingent cannot provide the full realm's strength at every frontier. Campaigns
consume provisions; distance and route reliability affect effective forces.
Losses remove actual people from recruitment bases. Fortifications and local
defenders remain relevant. Maintained fleets require crews and provisions and
limit how many troops can campaign across water.

Administrative demand is compared with personnel and local intermediaries,
rather than a universal maximum empire size. Territorial conquest, vassalage,
religious allegiance and provincial autonomy remain distinct. A defeated capital
is more likely to lead to direct annexation when the victor has the capacity to
absorb it; otherwise tributary arrangements are possible.

Collapse follows a feedback loop: declining collection → arrears → unpaid
services → weaker protection and local withholding → further loss of revenue.
Institutional health and accumulated strain change over years. Provincial
defections can precede rapid fragmentation after sustained breakdown. Civil
wars have a recovery interval. Knowledge, local organization, infrastructure,
officials, troops and reserves are partly inherited by successor states.

Better communication, trusted succession and funded administration increase
attainable resilience. Communication also makes organized rebellion possible
when the centre is weak. No technology grants immunity from collapse.

## Climate and historical validation

Default Earth rainfall and temperature use NOAA-hosted GPCC and GHCN-CAMS
1981–2010 normals. Missing numerical cells retain synthetic climate. Monthly
summaries inform seasonal temperature range, growing months and rainfall
seasonality. The previous weather, orbital monsoon and epoch mechanisms still
operate over the spatial baseline. See `src/CLIMATE.md` for exact provenance,
numerical import schema and limitations.

Reverse Engineer Anything could open the installed Climate Lab executable.
Its Godot resource directory is encrypted, and the connector did not support
that resource package. Its numerical climate algorithms were not recovered.
This version uses independently documented observational data and supports
numerical output imports with explicit grids and units; coloured screenshots
are not treated as climate measurements.

Checks now separate regional population, settlement size, administrative depth,
political coverage and organized populations outside states. The older
territorial-consolidation references remain visible as legacy anchors, with
their measurement definition made explicit. They are not exact first-state
dates or automatic pass/fail tests. Amazonian political coverage is not treated
as proof of low population or social simplicity.

Conservation tests, deterministic replay and seeded histories validate software
behaviour. They do not establish that the causal model or historical dates are
correct. A larger calibration study should use ensembles and held-out regions,
report parameter uncertainty, and compare mechanisms with transport,
institutional funding and ecological constraints switched off separately.
The current grid still smooths valleys, passes and urban hinterlands; a modern
climate normal is not a Holocene reconstruction.
