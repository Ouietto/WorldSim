# Asabiya 0.10 — civic growth, practical invention and coalition breakup

This version connects settlement, warfare, administration, reform and collapse
to shared resources. It is an exploratory historical model. Its rules are
explicit hypotheses, rather than established universal laws of social change.

## Pastoral submission, settlement and mixed government (v0.11)

A victorious pastoral polity can negotiate submission while leaving local
chiefs and territory in place, incorporate clans, or provoke an actual cohort
retreat. Accepted submission prevents automatic attacks on that subject.
Tribute uses existing shipments; wartime contingents use actual loaned troops,
provisions and return journeys. Protection, relative strength and continuity
influence allegiance. Subordination can end when the overlord weakens.

Displaced or insecure clans can petition reachable settled governments.
Acceptance needs spare livelihoods, food, suitable land or built urban capacity,
a maintained host and actual public provisions. Grants and travel have costs;
people, children, cultures, ancestry, immunity, herds and techniques move from
existing communities. New farming settlements have establishment delays.
Pastoral placement preserves access to pasture. Frontier agreements require
prior credible protection and do not annex the province or the migrants' home.

Durable settlement agreements track land rights, provisioning, protection and
confidence beyond one generation. Broken agreements can cause protest and
actual household departure. A capable government can negotiate smaller cohorts'
relocation to funded, accessible districts with spare livelihoods. This requires
local stores, administrators and service reliability, rather than a title.
Minority settlers are moved as the agreed cultural cohort. Existing farmers and
landholders reduce available settlement land. Coercive displacement remains a
separate existing war-law mechanism.

Managing multiple clans and subjects creates demand for administration. Actual
funding and practice can support trained officials; conquest gives no automatic
bureaucracy bonus. A mixed pastoral/agrarian administration needs sustained
agricultural revenue, viable towns and specialists, trained officials, local
cooperation, coalition acceptance and reliable provisions. Progress reverses
when those conditions fail. Transition adds political adjustment costs and
preserves mobile livelihoods; pastoral government can remain complex without
becoming agrarian. There is no automatic farmland-fraction conversion or age
reset that makes a coalition permanent.

## Urban residents and built capacity (v0.11)

Urban population represents actual residents rather than a target replaced at
each census. Local rural-to-urban movement and nearby household migration follow
employment, food, security, housing and water. Births and ordinary/disease deaths
change urban residents alongside the resident population. Decline returns some
residents to local rural livelihoods; migration moves actual households.

Households fund construction and upkeep from surplus. Public contributions use
discretionary local provisions after a payroll reserve. Built housing and water
capacity grow with paid work and construction limits, persist after a change of
government and deteriorate without maintenance. Natural water availability,
drought, local waterworks and maintained urban infrastructure limit density.
Sanitation and crowding modify enteric and respiratory transmission; investment
does not grant disease immunity. Sacking damages urban physical stocks.

Market, port, craft, administrative, ritual and frontier roles are readouts of
actual modeled activities, not randomly awarded city bonuses. Supporting towns
can specialize through connected markets. Local buyers compete for each farm
province's finite surplus, retaining producer subsistence and a buffer. Only
actual delivered grain contributes to imported urban provisioning; nearby cities
cannot all claim the same hypothetical hinterland surplus. Long-distance trade
still uses the maintained node/route network.

Water, housing, employment and migrant identities remain province aggregates.
Exact residential districts, property titles, individual job matching and a full
household genealogy are not modeled. Transition and settlement rates are
experimental and require historical calibration.

## Changes in 0.10

Cities can fund protection agreements with adjacent farming communities before
acquiring jurisdiction. Influence grows over decades through recurring local
provision and credible patrols. Troops, readiness, reserves, travel conditions
and mobile opposition constrain protection; walls around a city offer only
limited refuge. An incorporated community retains its residents, ancestry and
substantial autonomy. Officials and further provisions are required. A single
community cannot accrue several years of agreement from multiple neighboring
cities during one pass. Influence is an abstract share within the coarse map
cell, not a claim to survey individual farm boundaries.

Allied cities may accept protection when their interests support it. Leagues
and other dependencies can negotiate integration after sustained material
support and institutional guarantees. Strong civic identity slows acceptance;
rights, trustworthy government, proximity and administrative personnel matter.
Peaceful union transfers existing people, officials, forces, stocks and credit
obligations, preserving local identity and chartered autonomy. Broken promises
increase unrest and reduce local cooperation. Friendly relations alone do not
erase sovereignty; integration can stall or retreat.

City wall extent, condition and engineering knowledge are separate from
frontier fortifications. Local danger motivates construction, and paid works,
repairs and attacks build experience. Designs spread through actual exchange
or maintained local contact. Knowledge does not build free walls. Local
materials, available labor and recurring provisions limit realization. City
walls protect only a share of a province's population and do not block every
movement through its countryside. Sustained paid sieges can reduce urban
defenses; active siege pressure disrupts exchange. Granaries are a separate
built stock that improves storage and reduces spoilage. Walls create no food
or storage capacity. Civic works compete with harvest labor; public wages
return to resident households while material inputs are consumed.

Invention can be funded at supported cities throughout a polity, using each
location's actual resources. Projects and practical experience remain local,
including after a political border changes. Urban concentration gives a
bounded collaboration bonus; nutrition, disease, workshops and connected
exchange determine how much is realized. Recording, engineering, commerce
and military practice affect relevant projects. Territorial-size gates are
replaced by smaller accessible-population requirements and local practical
conditions. Prerequisites and material bottlenecks remain. Metallurgical
military advantages require working equipment produced and replaced from
provisions, alongside finite troops and training. Guns and transport animals
continue to require their own actual stocks. The equipment stock is a coarse
local proxy, not an inventory of each soldier's weapons or a complete arms market.

Confederation strain now responds more quickly across generations. Member-clan
loyalty feeds overall political cohesion. Failed rewards, hunger, succession
shocks and weak agreements raise breakup risk; delivered patronage, accepted
succession, shared danger and success can sustain older coalitions. Age raises
pressure without imposing a hundred-year expiration or automatically resetting
the founding date. Settled conquest dynasties use the state model after their
economic transition, rather than retaining permanent nomad status.

When a confederation dissolves, its public reserves remain with real communities.
Some actual households, children, provisions and herds move through bounded
local land routes into receptive cities or neighboring confederations. Other
households remain as autonomous clans. Acceptance depends on room, security
and relations; dispersal does not grant territory to a recipient. Joining
contingents transfer a share of existing troops, while the remaining command
disbands. Movement is recorded on the peoples map and carries culture,
ancestry, crop access and disease contact. New coalitions may form through the
ordinary local formation mechanisms; they are distinct political organizations.

The Largest control selects the current largest living polity, frames its
inhabited territory across wrapped longitude, and opens its inspector. Motion
is interruptible and respects reduced-motion preferences. An explicitly
inspected confederation is visible even when the general display filter hides it.
The deferred state, border and wall visual redesign remains deferred.

## Changes in 0.9

### Viable urban networks and civic credit

An urban population depends on actual harvests, arriving food, local crop
suitability, accessible hinterlands, storage and recurring administration.
Cold-season costs and damaged infrastructure reduce viability. Existing urban
centers encourage connected specialization through used trade routes or
maintained nodes; nearby centers also compete for food and workers. These
effects are staged, rather than spreading instantly across a continent.
Exports and general route traffic do not count as incoming food. There is no
Siberian ban or Mediterranean city bonus: supported ports, river settlements
and later transport can sustain urban life in otherwise difficult locations.

Civic loans transfer existing household surplus or a neighboring government's
reserves. External provisions travel as public consignments. Principal is a
claim, not another food stock. Repeated missed payments lower credit trust and
increase political vulnerability. Stronger neighbors may intervene, or a
government may exchange relief for protection. Default alone neither annexes
nor dissolves a city. Prolonged failure of food supply, collections and services
can dissolve an unsupported government while its inhabitants remain.
Rates and repayment terms are scenario parameters. This is a food-denominated
proxy, not a legal reconstruction of ancient credit or a monetary banking model.

### Frontier assembly and maritime foundations

Actual state incursions spread local threat information among connected
pastoral communities. Shared danger can encourage a defensive confederation
or accession to an existing one, provided people, provisions, herds and
leadership can support it. Alliances and protection agreements can discourage
assembly against a patron. Threat memory fades; a state nearby alone does not
guarantee a large confederation.

Long-lived coastal city states can fund maritime practice and merchant fleets.
Land pressure, exclusion, political insecurity and commercial opportunity
encourage expeditions. Ships, food, emigrants, livestock, officials and escorts
come from actual source stocks. Sites must be accessible and suitable for
known crops or local water. Voyages take time and face mortality or rejection.
Accepted settlers can establish independent daughter polities, dependencies or
enclaves within an existing host. Nearby communities gain contact and incentives
to coordinate; they do not automatically become states. Maritime colonization
does not grant the adjacent hinterland for free. Naval logistics and indigenous
agreements remain coarse aggregate rules.

### Regional peoples, movement and ruined cities

Initial macro naming families have smaller regional branches. Connected
communities can develop distinct identities after long isolation without
rewriting ancestry. Non-state foragers and pastoralists deplete and revisit
renewable seasonal grounds, moving portions of their actual households and
herds. Migrating coalitions can leave individual contingents at different
locations while others continue. The peoples map shows resident minorities,
recent camp traces and settlements left along a migration route.

Nomadic victories and raids can damage cities, kill inhabitants and drive
survivors into accounted refugee cohorts. Motives and intended dynastic rule
affect the probability of destruction. Ruins retain their former city identity;
repair needs sustained provisions and organization. A functional government can
relocate its capital, while an already failing government may shatter.
There is no automatic destruction of every conquered city. Culture and ancestry
still have only two resident components per province, and refugee pools remain
aggregated by cultural affiliation rather than individual households.

### Epidemics and institutional strain

Five stylized pathogen families replace a universal immunity score. Four
affect people; one removes actual working livestock. Family-specific exposed,
infectious and recovered fractions advance in 24 shorter steps inside a year.
Incubation, infectious duration, waning immunity, crowding, nutrition and
environmental suitability matter. Parameter values describe experimental
scenarios, not measured historical pathogens or reconstructed pandemic dates.

Used trade routes, caravans, local contact, armies, settlers and migrations
create delayed infection contacts. Infection survival falls with journey
length, although sizeable groups can sustain a transmission chain. Clearing
forest can increase spillover opportunities; suitable climate still constrains
vector transmission. A previously unexposed population can suffer repeated
introductions from several families without a scripted regional death event.
Large connected populations both sustain transmission and replenish susceptible
cohorts. Containment depends on trust, organization, public services and actual
provisions, and reduces exchange. Illness lowers labor; deaths remove real
people, officials, troops or animals. Revenue loss and succession shocks feed
the existing political model rather than an automatic collapse switch.

Journeys carry expected infection doses, not individually simulated patients.
Seasonality, host species and within-city contacts are simplified. Incoming
residents mix immunity, but mobile cohorts do not run their own full epidemic
compartments. Birth replacement is approximated by annual immunity dilution.
These rules need multi-seed epidemiological and political calibration.

### Map input

Touch, pen and mouse share pointer handling. Two fingers pinch around their
moving midpoint; lifting one finger continues panning without a jump. Cancelled
contacts do not select provinces. Double tap provides an additional touch zoom
gesture. This release changes input behavior and peoples information overlays;
the deferred state, border and wall aesthetic redesign remains deferred.

## Changes in 0.8

### Crops, highlands and dry plateaus

Farming knowledge and access to a crop package are separate. Six broad packages
have thermal, moisture, altitude and storage properties. Hearths supply local
crops; staged contact, trade, settlers and migrants carry them elsewhere.
Adaptation takes time and provisions. Rotation remains a separate technique.
Potential farmland alone cannot supply an absent crop.

Coarse mountainous cells retain a share of usable valley ground. Highland
tubers and quinoa have different tolerances from lowland crops. Locally funded
terraces, runoff works, storage and porter or llama transport support highland
intensification. Local catchments can support dry plateau farming without a
major river. Coastal fishing supplies another subsistence base. These rules
make Andean and Iranian environments more viable without spawning named states
or suppressing Pampas agriculture by decree. Valleys, aquifers, irrigation and
crop varieties remain simplified; there is no reconstructed qanat network.

Workshop activity, record practice and practical chemical experience constrain
advanced inventions. Projects share a paid specialist-work budget within a
polity. Knowledge of gunpowder provides no military bonus without actual
weapons produced and maintained from local resources. Technology chronology
needs further calibration; these thresholds are scenario parameters.

### Local reserves and exposed frontiers

Provincial taxes enter local depots; capital stores are held separately.
Garrisons, payroll, relief, wall upkeep and frontier campaigns draw on reserves
where they operate. Public consignments share trade-edge capacity with private
food traffic and suffer losses; uncontrolled intermediate territory blocks
government freight. Tributary and protection payments travel as shipments.
Captured stores stay at their physical location, including after capital
relocation. This is annual staged freight, rather than a complete transport
market or a daily supply simulation.

Open pasture can be fertile and still be dangerous to settle. Mobile resistance
depends on actual people, herds, riding animals, organization, experience and
provisioning. Forest, ruggedness, rivers, funded defenses and trained forces
modify exposure. Both autonomous pastoral communities and confederations can
resist occupation. There is no ethnic toughness constant or universal ban on
pre-gunpowder states. Forested Russia, open steppe and river enclaves therefore
face different conditions. Seasonal pasture and harsh winter shocks affect
herd survival; severe drought can destroy the means of military expansion.

Raids can reach beyond the immediate border along limited accessible routes,
seize local stores, disrupt harvests and damage infrastructure. Repeated loss
of a weak government's administrative center can release its provinces
without annexing them or erasing their inhabitants. Wealth, local political
divisions, ambition and spare occupation capacity can also motivate an
opportunistic state intervention. Threatened neighbors can strengthen their
defense arrangements.

### Walls, redirection and migration cascades

Walls require local annual maintenance. Poor repairs, weak patrols and scouting
can expose a breach. An opened gap reduces the barrier for subsequent movement
until repaired. A route planner does not create a breach: an actual funded
campaign or moving group must force it.

Mobile coalitions scout a bounded network of connected pasture and crossings.
Route costs include forage, supplies, terrain, rivers, opposing forces, walls
and pressure from other movements. Intact defenses can redirect a movement
around their ends; an existing gap can become the cheapest passage. The
algorithm has no preferred compass direction and cannot cross an impassable
wall merely because its goal lies beyond it. Scouting remains a coarse proxy
for geographic knowledge rather than a detailed information network.

Encountered nomads can fight, contribute actual households and herds, or
withdraw in a new migration. Pressure decays; retreat has a cooldown and the
number of active parties is bounded. Success and practical experience can
attract more participants. Neither a cascade nor a successful invasion is
guaranteed. Food shortages, casualties, grazing, command and resisting
coalitions constrain the process. Territory, population movement and cultural
change remain distinct.

### Protection, foreign service and coalition renewal

A centralized government with a history of raids can promise regular
provisions to a confederation. Actual shipments support the pact. Arrears or a
large shift in power can produce renewed war. A victorious confederation can
install a conquest dynasty only when forces, reserves, weak incumbent
institutions and cooperation from local intermediaries permit it.

States at war can engage warriors from nearby or allied confederations. A
contract earmarks existing troops, removes them from the provider's available
forces, has travel time, and needs recurring payment. Losses remove people
from the original recruitment base. Returning veterans retain bounded tactical
experience and knowledge of techniques actually encountered. They can carry
equipment removed from the employer's stocks. Partial knowledge is not full
adoption. Dependents remain at home during temporary service; individual
soldier journeys and military households are not simulated.

Confederations accumulate strain as personal bargains span generations.
Reward disputes, succession, member-clan loyalty and command capacity matter.
Actual provisions received from settled patrons can support their renewal.
This makes long survival harder under weak bargains without an automatic
hundred-year expiration. Patronage is no guarantee of loyalty or permanence.

### Coercion and demographic consequences

Conquest laws can permit expulsion; rarer collective-reprisal policies can
allow selective adult-male mortality after severe resistance. These actions
need occupation capacity and provisions and cause economic damage, distrust,
defense mobilization, displacement and demographic losses. Refugee cohorts
carry real people, food and herds. Sponsored replacement settlers must come
from actual neighboring populations. Sustained funded coercion and contact
can shift a local cultural majority over one or two generations; this is
distinct from killing or instant ancestry replacement.

Provincial culture and ancestry use separate two-component summaries. Their
joint household distribution is not known, so selective removal of ancestry
is approximate. Adult sex composition is an aggregate share with gradual
recovery, recruitment consequences and a fertility penalty after extreme
imbalance; there are no full age-by-sex cohorts. These simplifications limit
what the model can claim about any particular historical displacement.

### Map presentation

Map layers use one dropdown. In States and Alliances, confederations can be
shown, hidden, or shown when near substantial settled communities, at war
with a state, or in contact through trade. Qualifying coalitions appear as a
whole, with ten years of visibility grace. Fills, borders, labels and direct
polity selection follow the filter. Cities and names remain independent.
Changing visibility never changes ownership, random draws or simulation rules.

## Changes in 0.7

### Herds, traction and carrying capacity

Six livestock populations are separate from technical knowledge: cattle,
water buffalo, donkeys, horses, camels and llamas. They have different traction,
pack and riding contributions, habitat suitability and breeding rates.
Historical mode uses approximate domestication scenario anchors; generated
worlds choose suitable origins. These dates and locations are not a new
archaeological chronology. Knowing riding or herding does not create breeders.
Founder placement searches for a viable nearby habitat. River water can support
buffalo in an otherwise dry district, and camels can use arid scrub that is not
temperate pasture.
Animals diffuse through staged local exchange and trade links, with food
payments transferred between households. Breeding and exchange phases have a separate conservation ledger;
settlement and migration transfers are checked by focused scenarios. Settlers, displaced groups and mobile coalitions carry actual
animals from their origins. Animal breeding and exchange run in five-year
steps, so new animal availability can take years to affect a route.

Fodder competes with household food and is explicitly subtracted in the annual
food ledger. Grazing suitability, crowding, weather and feed shortages affect
herd survival. Draft capacity limits plough benefits. Pack supply lowers the
population and occupation requirements of a frontier; boats and organized
human labor provide alternatives. Forest is a transport and husbandry cost,
not a universal livestock exclusion. Buffalo can support wet Asian agriculture.
Earth's coarse tsetse habitat proxy is confined to mainland Africa; generated
worlds retain a hypothetical climate-defined disease habitat.

### Communication corridors and natural frontiers

Order delivery, replies and bulk supplies follow separate directed routes.
Distances are estimated travel days, quantized into half-day steps. Routing
uses maintained roads, real remount availability, relay knowledge, terrain,
river direction and coastal shipping. Local river craft can predate developed
seafaring. Major channels are inferred from drainage, gradient and roughness;
low flow, seasonal rainfall and cold reduce reliability. Canal knowledge can
help river bottlenecks. This remains a coarse grid proxy, not a surveyed map
of navigable channels or simulated monthly vessel movements.

A state's reach depends on round-trip response and supply burden, rather than
one universal geographic radius. Connected waterways create demand for
central offices and standardization. They do not exempt a polity from budgets,
local bargaining or succession. Mountains and transverse river crossings
increase attack and travel costs. Longitudinal river movement can be cheap at
the same location. Bridges reduce crossing friction. Defensible frontiers
require fewer recurring occupation personnel; valley routes and passes remain
potential approaches. No named river, mountain range or region receives a
scripted frontier or cohesion bonus.

### City states, leagues and conquest

Urban polities with small territorial hinterlands can be classified as city
states. Urban populations, civic identities and memories of previous local
rule persist through conquest. They lower the initial ability of an outsider
to integrate a city. Alliances of small states can become leagues that retain
local councils and laws; these are tributary/federal approximations, not a
complete model of a historical constitution. Membership can lapse if relative
power or protection changes. Leagues are not silently annexed by the ordinary
vassal-absorption rule.

Acquiring provinces creates conquest obligations. Recent, poorly integrated
populations provide few recruits. Obligations decay through time, reliable
services and bureaucratic integration, while increasing fiscal strain. The
death of a successful conqueror can expose personal arrangements that were
not converted into trusted succession institutions. Fragmentation can restore
local political identities or produce successor governments. A city-state era
is an emergent regional possibility, not a mandatory global sequence.

### Peace, institutional memory and elite interests

Preparedness, retained crisis experience and frontier intelligence are separate
stocks. Training consumes provisions. Record keeping, administrative schools
and continuity preserve knowledge; maintained preparation can improve during
peace. Severe losses remove expertise. There is no fixed old-age penalty or
rule that stable borders inevitably cause collapse. Raiding coalitions learn
about particular opponents, but knowledge fades and repeated defeats lower a
target's appeal. Skills have ceilings and both sides learn.

Provincial landholders have local stakes and loyalties. Reliable protection,
rights and secure property can make them defend the existing order. Failed
protection and credible rival terms can induce a local change of allegiance.
This transfers jurisdiction and a limited actual contingent, not ethnicity or
population. Retained local authority strengthens intermediaries and limits
central extraction. Existing costly reforms can replace some of that power
only when supporting institutions and coalitions exist. Elites and estates
remain aggregate provincial proxies, not individually simulated families.

### Pastoral coalitions and migration

Pastoral territories are difficult for sedentary armies to occupy, especially
when residents can evade them using herds and riding animals. Dense urban
agricultural enclaves remain possible. Nomadic coalitions can form around local
organization, pasture conditions, threats and access to settled wealth.
Spatial clan groups track membership and loyalty; failed protection and reward
distribution can produce departures and successor coalitions. They have no
fixed lifespan. The spatial groups are political proxies, not a reconstruction
of kinship. Nomad-born rulers can still become settled conquest dynasties.

Raiding failures can redirect attacks or encourage migration. Migrating groups
explore accessible pasture corridors and local opportunities instead of knowing
all world capitals. Movement costs food, entails casualties, carries animals,
and can recruit actual households from communities along the route. Mobile
coalitions retain multiple population cohorts, can change direction, and can
persist for decades. Group identity is not treated as proof of uniform ancestry.
Repeated pressure can destabilize states without guaranteeing their defeat.

States can admit newcomers when they have room, provisions and a political
basis for agreement. An initial grant and annual provisions draw from actual
reserves. Fulfilled agreements support limited auxiliary recruitment. Sustained
broken promises can generate unrest and an armed departure drawn from the
settled population. Admission, redirection, negotiated settlement, violence and
state formation are distinct possible outcomes. Cohort ancestry/culture is
still compressed into the existing two-component provincial representation.

### Coastal settlement and map display

Mild, adequately watered coastal districts can support investment in productive
export crops. Maintained water transport, local nodes, urban demand, public
investment and time determine its benefit. This makes suitable North African
coasts attractive under the same rules as comparable coasts elsewhere. It
does not turn desert into farmland or treat inhabited land as empty.

City display can be hidden, restricted to capitals, limited to major regional
cities plus capitals, or expanded to all surviving towns. Importance uses
estimated urban population rather than rural province population. Government
seats remain visible in capital modes. Symbols and names avoid overlaps;
small-city names require zooming in. Filters change presentation only.

## Food, labour and population

One food unit is one person's annual ration. Provinces have a potential
carrying capacity, an actual annual harvest, household provisions, children and
a nutrition level. Harvests depend on available workers and local capacity;
knowing farming does not immediately produce a full-capacity harvest. Soldiers,
officials and fleet crews draw labour away from production.

Governments collect a portion of actual production. Collection depends on
administrative reach, integration, local autonomy, compliance and the resources
that can be appropriated. Livestock and labour contributions remain possible
without cereal farming. Taxes leave household stores and enter public stores.
Salaries return food to the same population counted in household consumption.
Tribute transfers existing provisions rather than producing an overlord bonus.

Stores spoil. Public reserves pay salaries, maintenance, relief, training,
campaign provisioning, colonies and reform. Insufficient revenue produces
arrears and unpaid services. Relief has a delivery cost and can fail to reach
distant provinces. Food conservation is checked across each annual economy
pass, including fodder, transportation losses and spoilage.

Births, ordinary deaths, starvation and an eighteen-year maturation pipeline
replace the single logistic population update. Scarce food reduces growth;
children cannot immediately become recruits or trained officials. People in
migration parties and displaced groups are included in population statistics.

## Settlements and frontiers

Migrants are removed from their actual home populations. Provisions, seed and
establishment inputs are deducted before departure. Clearing forest, difficult
terrain and longer journeys cost more. Newcomers produce only part of their
potential harvest during establishment; their presence does not suppress the
entire established population's harvest. Underfed colonies can fail and return
people to their home region.

Households can migrate independently. States can finance larger colonies and
must have enough local population, collected provisions, administrative
authority, service reliability and occupation capacity to sponsor a frontier.
Sea crossings require provisioning too. Claiming an empty cell no longer
creates settlers. A large population somewhere in an empire is insufficient
when provisions and protection cannot be delivered to the frontier.

A state with spare administrative capacity can also sponsor colonists after
conquering an inhabited frontier. This moves and provisions actual households;
jurisdiction alone does not replace the local population. The settlement counter
counts newly peopled provinces, while sponsored movements also include settlement
among existing inhabitants. The grid cannot distinguish every new village
inside a province roughly a hundred kilometres across.

Conquest of an existing community and founding an empty settlement are different
operations. Forests, rough country, local organization and disease resist
occupation. Cereal share is no longer a near-veto on conquest or retention.
Integration, administration and recurring occupation commitments constrain
expansion into populated territory.

## Trade and communication

Ports, navigable rivers, confluences, maintained road junctions, cities and
caravan stops create trade nodes. A network is rebuilt periodically from these
conditions. Small local exchanges are possible away from nodes, with much lower
capacity. River and maritime links allow larger flows. Actual food moves from
stocks with surplus to places with demand, subject to throughput, safety,
transport losses and tolls. Wars interrupt links.

Subsistence practices can spread locally without a long-distance commercial
network. The transmission of more specialized skills is more dependent on
nodes, contact, maintained routes and actual exchange. This avoids making the
spread of farming depend on already-existing cities or states.

This remains a food-equivalent economy. Apart from the civic-loan proxy above,
it does not separately simulate prices, general commercial credit, ore deposits
or inventories of every traded commodity.
River routes are simplified, and passing through several exchange links is
resolved within an annual step rather than as individual caravans and ships.

## Communities, conquest and cultural change

Communal organization builds through repeated investment, surplus, shared
infrastructure and coordination needs. Councils, granaries and local
sanctuaries can exist outside states. Formal polities emerge only after local
organization and a supportable population develop. Council, temple and
patrimonial governments can start with different political arrangements.

Alliances of non-state communities can raid and conquer neighbours, including
state provinces. They gather local contingents and provisions, face resistance,
take losses, displace residents and move actual settlers. Successful warbands
can launch larger coercive migrations. Migration parties negotiate or fight;
settling in one location does not automatically annex neighbouring provinces.

Displaced people retain food and remain accounted for while they await movement.
They may migrate, suffer mortality or resettle. Hordes moving out withdraw
people, children and supplies from their former territory.

Language/cultural affiliation and ancestry are recorded separately. Settlement
can change both; assimilation can change culture without rewriting ancestry.
Culture changes by shares and can form hybrids. A culture can lose its final
communities through repeated displacement, mortality and assimilation, without
a universal extermination event or a scripted Indo-European replacement.
Ancestry is a coarse two-component population label, not a genetic simulation.

Political identity can persist across language boundaries. A state's heirs
inherit its political tradition; unrelated populations can participate through
long integration and reliable institutions. Safe interiors do not automatically
lose all cohesion: trust, negotiated rights and maintained administration can
support cooperation.

## Institutions, coalitions and succession

Added techniques include communal storage, accounts and record keeping,
censuses and land surveys, messenger relays, salaried public offices,
recognized succession procedures, negotiated charters, navigable canals and
schools for administrators. Record keeping includes non-alphabetic alternatives.
The older universal bronze → writing → wheel → iron prerequisite chain has
been relaxed.

Technologies permit capabilities; trained personnel and recurring support
determine how much is realized. Bureaucratic skill builds gradually. Roads and
canals are maintained stocks, distinct from knowledge of construction.
Administrative distance includes terrain, roads, rivers and shipping. A
connected transport network creates incentives for standards and central
offices, without named bonuses for China or the Mediterranean.

Nobles, officials, merchants and clergy have different sources of power and
different grounds for supporting government. Hereditary intermediaries provide
local coordination and military support while retaining revenue and limiting
central collection. Negotiated rights can improve loyalty while constraining
the ruler. Provincial organization and communications can support defection
when salaries, protection and central authority fail.

A reforming ruler needs competence, reserves, trained replacements, a
supporting coalition and an opportunity. Reform consumes substantial provisions
and initially disrupts the state. Its changes to offices, authority and
intermediary power outlive the individual ruler. Reform is uncommon, and it
does not guarantee permanent success. State revenue and total economic output
are separate measures.

Succession procedures can sustain hereditary continuity; councils select among
candidates. Low legitimacy, excluded elites and weak supporting coalitions
increase usurpation risk. A dynastic overthrow can leave the territorial and
administrative state intact.

Coalitions are aggregate organized groups rather than individually simulated
households and estates. The model does not yet maintain a complete genealogy,
legal system, named provincial governor population or historical constitution.

## Warfare, fleets and collapse

Armies recruit gradually from an integrated and locally supported adult recruitment base. Recently conquered provinces contribute very few recruits. Frontier occupation also requires spare trained personnel or local intermediaries after existing administrative commitments are met; cash alone cannot create unlimited occupation capacity. Offensive forces
and defensive reserves are finite allocations for the current year. A
contingent cannot provide the full realm's strength at every frontier. Campaigns
consume provisions; distance and route reliability affect effective forces.
Losses remove actual people from recruitment bases. Fortifications and local
defenders remain relevant. Maintained fleets require crews and provisions and
limit how many troops can campaign across water.

Administrative demand is compared with personnel and local intermediaries,
rather than a universal maximum empire size. Territorial conquest, vassalage,
religious allegiance and provincial autonomy remain distinct. A defeated capital
is more likely to lead to direct annexation when the victor has the capacity to
absorb it; otherwise tributary arrangements are possible.

Collapse follows a feedback loop: declining collection → arrears → unpaid
services → weaker protection and local withholding → further loss of revenue.
Institutional health and accumulated strain change over years. Provincial
defections can precede rapid fragmentation after sustained breakdown. Civil
wars have a recovery interval. Knowledge, local organization, infrastructure,
officials, troops and reserves are partly inherited by successor states.

Better communication, trusted succession and funded administration increase
attainable resilience. Communication also makes organized rebellion possible
when the centre is weak. No technology grants immunity from collapse.

## Climate and historical validation

Default Earth rainfall and temperature use NOAA-hosted GPCC and GHCN-CAMS
1981–2010 normals. Missing numerical cells retain synthetic climate. Monthly
summaries inform seasonal temperature range, growing months and rainfall
seasonality. The previous weather, orbital monsoon and epoch mechanisms still
operate over the spatial baseline. See `src/CLIMATE.md` for exact provenance,
numerical import schema and limitations.

Reverse Engineer Anything could open the installed Climate Lab executable.
Its Godot resource directory is encrypted, and the connector did not support
that resource package. Its numerical climate algorithms were not recovered.
This version uses independently documented observational data and supports
numerical output imports with explicit grids and units; coloured screenshots
are not treated as climate measurements.

Checks now separate regional population, settlement size, administrative depth,
political coverage and organized populations outside states. The older
territorial-consolidation references remain visible as legacy anchors, with
their measurement definition made explicit. They are not exact first-state
dates or automatic pass/fail tests. Amazonian political coverage is not treated
as proof of low population or social simplicity.

Conservation tests, deterministic replay and seeded histories validate software
behaviour. They do not establish that the causal model or historical dates are
correct. A larger calibration study should use ensembles and held-out regions,
report parameter uncertainty, and compare mechanisms with transport,
institutional funding and ecological constraints switched off separately.
The current grid still smooths valleys, passes and urban hinterlands; a modern
climate normal is not a Holocene reconstruction.
